# 22 campaign principles

Adapted from the user-provided Claude skill, which attributes its framework to
Marc Kuchner's *Marketing for Scientists* (2011). These are editorial adaptations,
not independently verified quotations, chapter attributions, or validated hiring
predictors. Consult the original source before attributing a specific claim to it.

| # | Principle | Practical test |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Write for the reader's decision | Can each main claim be connected to a selection criterion? |
| 2 | Consider the relevant audiences | Can specialists, the chair, teaching colleagues, and referees understand the contribution they need to assess? |
| 3 | Resolve material objections | Is a legitimate ambiguity clarified with evidence, rather than hidden by praise? |
| 4 | Make the identity repeatable | Can a colleague restate the contribution accurately in one sentence? |
| 5 | Start with a familiar problem | Can a reader outside the narrow method area understand the opening? |
| 6 | Differentiate honestly | Does the distinction hold without an unsupported first/best claim? |
| 7 | Give the work a coherent emphasis | Is there a clear through-line, while retaining breadth when the role values it? |
| 8 | Name a real signature contribution | Is the name consistent and useful, and is collaborative credit accurate? |
| 9 | Assess the future of each strand | Is there evidence for its remaining questions and relevance, beyond fashion or citation counts? |
| 10 | Show agency with accurate context | Are actions and contributions clear without erasing constraints or career breaks? |
| 11 | Evidence the role's resource needs | Are grant roles and feasible plans clear where funding matters? Is teaching given its advertised weight? |
| 12 | Offer inspectable work | Can an appropriate reader inspect a paper, teaching resource, method, or reusable artefact without violating access or release restrictions? |
| 13 | Use figures with a purpose | Does each figure explain the problem, locate the contribution, or show a fair comparison? |
| 14 | Connect past evidence to future value | Is the proposed contribution to this department specific and feasible? |
| 15 | Match questions to feasible methods | Are the research questions, methods, resources, uncertainty, and milestones connected? |
| 16 | Check public information | Are visible titles, dates, publication status, and links accurate? |
| 17 | Keep a useful research site | Can a visitor find current work and contact information quickly? |
| 18 | Prefer useful interaction to volume | Does the activity serve a relevant audience within the time budget? No fixed posting ratio is required. |
| 19 | Follow up meaningfully | Is the follow-up specific, welcome, and proportionate to the interaction? |
| 20 | Build informed professional relationships | Do potential referees or collaborators actually know the work? A fixed follower count is not a hiring criterion. |
| 21 | Make genuine, manageable requests | Is the request useful and respectful, with room to decline, rather than a tactic to create obligation? |
| 22 | Check whether the case is memorable | Can someone explain the candidate's contribution and fit without exaggeration? |

## Figures and talks

The source skill's three figure types translate to an orienting visual, a field
map, and a before/after comparison. Choose those that serve the argument and fit
the format; none is mandatory. Comparisons need compatible baselines, denominators,
and stated limitations. Conceptual future results must be labelled as such.

A job talk should establish the problem, contribution, evidence, technical depth,
limitations, future programme, and local fit. Tailor the sequence to the audience
and allotted time. Explain difficult material clearly; deliberate obscurity is
not evidence of expertise. Answer questions directly, acknowledge uncertainty,
and return to the main point only after addressing the question.
