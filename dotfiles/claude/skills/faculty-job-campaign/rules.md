# Rules

22 operational rules for a faculty job campaign, distilled from Marc Kuchner,
*Marketing for Scientists* (Island Press, 2011). Every rule traces to a chapter.
Where the 2011 text needed changing for the current academic market, the change
is marked **[ADAPTED]** with the reason.

Each rule carries a concrete test. A rule you cannot test against an artefact is
not usable in a review.

Effort and impact ratings assume a postdoc applying in the next two cycles.

---

## A. Audience: who actually decides

### Rule 1. Write every asset from the reader's side of the desk

Before drafting anything, write down what the panel is worrying about, then
draft against that list. The book's exercise is exact: the concerns of a scholar
*writing* a paper (error bars, figure placement, coauthor sign-off, getting
scooped) share nothing with the concerns of a scholar *reading* one (did they
cite me, does this support my hypothesis, can I use this in my next proposal).
The same gap separates a candidate writing an application from a panellist
reading one.

**Test.** Ask for the four concerns list. If the draft was written without one,
it was written from the candidate's side.

**Trace.** Ch 2, the movie-theatre and scholar exercises. Ch 3, WIIFM.
**Effort** 30 minutes per asset. **Impact** high.

### Rule 2. Name your four consumers separately

They need different sentences from you.

| Consumer | Main concern | What they need to see |
|---|---|---|
| Search chair | Does this person fill the gap we advertised | Explicit mapping to the advertised criteria, in their words |
| Head of school or department | Teaching load covered, money coming in | Courses you can take over from day one, funding you are eligible for |
| Outside panel member | Is this person known, can I defend them | A recognisable named thing, venues they respect, people they know |
| Referees | Can I write superlatives without lying | Material that lets them write "best" or "top two or three" |

**Test.** For each of the four, name the one sentence in the application aimed at
them. Any consumer with no sentence is unserved.

**Trace.** Ch 7, the consumer taxonomy (students, junior scientists, senior
scientists, funding-agency staff, press officers, reporters, public,
policymakers), each with a stated main concern.
**[ADAPTED]** The book's consumers are the scientific community at large. Recast
to a hiring panel, which the book treats only in passing.
**Effort** 1 hour. **Impact** high.

### Rule 3. Committees eliminate before they select

Negative impressions weigh roughly five to seven times more than positive ones.
The book is explicit about the consequence on panels: one strongly negative
comment leaves a stain that several people must override, decisions get made on
fewest-negatives rather than most-outstanding, and so "if you are writing a
proposal or applying for a job, and you expect you might have to impress a
committee, your task might be more about eliminating negatives than about
dazzling people."

**Test.** The elimination audit. List every objection a hostile panellist could
raise in 30 seconds from the CV alone. Each one gets pre-empted, fixed, or
deliberately accepted with a route around it.

**Trace.** Ch 3, enthusiasm and optimism, and the panel-size discussion.
**Effort** 1 hour. **Impact** high. This is the highest-yield hour in a campaign.

## B. Positioning: the line they repeat when you are not in the room

### Rule 4. Own one sentence

Not a topic list. One repeatable identity in the form "the one who does X for Y".
The book's version is Ries and Trout's law five: the most powerful concept in
marketing is owning a word in the prospect's mind. Scientists have an unusual
licence here, because a term a scientist coins can become an actual word.

**Test.** Say the line to someone outside the subfield. Ask them to repeat it an
hour later. If they cannot, it is not owned.

**Trace.** Ch 5, Ries and Trout Law #5.
**[ADAPTED]** For interdisciplinary candidates, the line must also carry a home
discipline anchor: which department hires this person. The 2011 book assumes a
single-discipline career and does not anticipate the hybrid candidate who reads
as neither engineer nor computer scientist and loses both shortlists.
**Effort** days, iterated. **Impact** high.

### Rule 5. Position against what they already know

Not in a vacuum. Avis did not claim to be best; it said "Avis is only No. 2, we
try harder", and anchored itself on Hertz, which everyone already knew. The
book's own pitch works the same way: "You know the Hubble Space Telescope?" then
attach.

**Test.** The first clause of the pitch names something the panel already knows.
If the first clause is your own method name, the anchor is missing.

**Trace.** Ch 3, positioning.
**Effort** hours. **Impact** high.

### Rule 6. If you cannot be first in a category, define the category you are first in

The 352nd exoplanet gets no applause. The first planet a press release called a
super-Neptune gets National Geographic. The intersection claim beats the broad
claim, and the honest intersection is usually available.

**Test.** Write the claim with the word "first" in it. If the sentence is false,
narrow the category until it is true, then check that the narrowed category is
one a committee cares about. A category no one wants to be first in is not a
position.

**Trace.** Ch 5, Ries and Trout Law #2.
**Effort** hours. **Impact** high.

### Rule 7. Do not dabble

You cannot run one brand across two unrelated products. For a campaign window,
one lead strand. Everything else is supporting evidence, never a co-headline.
The book quotes Sean Carroll: work in a second area "will be compared to the
best people in the world who spend all their time thinking about area B, and you
will probably come up wanting". Ries and Trout's law thirteen is the general
form: you have to give up something to get something.

**Test.** Read the research statement's first paragraph and the CV's top third.
Count the strands presented as equals. More than one is dabbling.

**Trace.** Ch 5, Law #13, and the Sean Carroll quotation.
**Effort** the decision is an hour, the consequences are permanent for a cycle.
**Impact** high.

### Rule 8. Have a Signature Research Idea, and give it a name

A named research tool or research question that becomes, in the book's phrase,
your résumé in a nutshell. "A hiring committee looking at your record doesn't
have to work too hard to justify hiring you. All they need to do is say you're
the one with a certain SRI." Best if it carries your name, but a name it does
not already share with a hundred other groups will do.

The name must be the same everywhere: CV, talk, website, repository, every
application. A thing with three names has none.

**Test.** Search the candidate's own materials for the name. Count the variants.
Then search the name on the open web and see whether the candidate owns the
first page.

**Trace.** Ch 8, the Signature Research Idea. Ch 5, the band-limited mask story,
where three papers and a talk at every relevant conference were what turned an
invention into a brand.
**Effort** naming is hours; establishing is a year of consistency.
**Impact** high. The book identifies this as the usual route to a junior faculty
hire.

### Rule 9. Brands expire. Know where each strand sits on its curve

Interest in a research product rises, saturates, and falls. When work feels less
exciting and more effortful, the brand may have peaked. Do not lead with a
saturated strand. Successful scientists keep a small portfolio at different
stages.

Note the book's own caveat against over-applying this: sticking with the PhD
topic is only a mistake if the PhD was ordinary. If it launched something, stay.

**Test.** For each strand: citations trend, whether invitations still arrive,
whether new groups have entered. A strand where the field has moved on is
supporting evidence, not the headline.

**Trace.** Ch 5, Law #19. Ch 15, the product life-cycle curve and "doing his PhD
over and over".
**Effort** an afternoon. **Impact** medium.

### Rule 10. Project hero and creator. Never the orphan

Letters of recommendation are written in the language of the hero and warrior
archetype: best, top, one of the top two or three. Leadership letters need the
ruler; creativity letters need the creator. A candidate who presents as the
orphan, the archetype whose life happens to them and who complains without
taking responsibility, makes that language unavailable to the referee and
signals unreadiness for a grant or students.

**Test.** Scan the CV, statements, and public posts for orphan grammar: passive
constructions about your own career, circumstance-blaming, apologising for the
record, "I was not given the opportunity to". Then check the inverse: is there
anything in the file a referee could quote as a superlative?

**Trace.** Ch 6, archetypes; the orphan as the shadow of the innocent; the
recommendation-letter passage.
**Effort** hours. **Impact** high, and it is invisible to the candidate, which
is why it needs an external check.

## C. Products: what you are actually selling

### Rule 11. The product is grant capacity, not papers

The book's central reframing: papers, talks and conference proceedings are
marketing tools; proposals are the product, because proposals are what money is
exchanged for. A junior scientist's job is to show the people who might hire them
how useful they will be for developing and winning proposals. "If you are crucial
for proposals, you must be a crucial person to hire."

**Test.** Find the funding evidence in the CV without searching. Proposals
written, agencies, roles, budget lines, named calls you are eligible for as PI.
If a reader has to infer it, it is not there.

**Trace.** Ch 8, the proposal supply chain.
**[ADAPTED]** Stronger now than in 2011. UK, Irish, Dutch and Portuguese
advertisements increasingly name grant capture as an explicit criterion, and
eligibility for a specific first-PI scheme (ERC Starting, MSCA, IRC, SFI, NWO
Veni, FCT CEEC) is checkable by the panel. State the scheme and the eligibility
window explicitly.
**Effort** hours to surface, months to build. **Impact** high.

### Rule 12. Give away free samples of the real product

Papers are presumed free and score nothing. A free sample means giving away a
piece of the actual product: a research tool, software, a catalogue, a dataset,
an equation someone can use without reading the paper, with your name and your
SRI's name on it. The white paper is the free sample of a proposal: one to four
pages, section headings, a figure with a caption, written for a PhD outside your
field, with white space.

The book notes that the three-page essays requested in faculty and fellowship
applications are white papers in disguise. A research statement is a white paper.

**Test.** Name the artefact a stranger can use today without asking you for
anything. If there is none, that is the gap.

**Trace.** Ch 8, free samples, research tools, white papers.
**[ADAPTED]** Open code and public datasets now do this work better than the
book's 2011 examples, and they are checkable. A maintained repository with a
readable README is the strongest free sample available to a computational
candidate.
**Effort** days per artefact. **Impact** high.

### Rule 13. Build three figures and reuse them everywhere

Every proposal, talk and statement needs three kinds of figure, and readers in a
hurry look at figures first.

| Figure | Job | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| **Beautiful Butterfly** | Attention. A striking image carrying no quantitative load. Better if it carries none. | Opening slide, top of the research statement, journal cover submission |
| **Family Portrait** | Places the field and flatters it. Everyone's data or method on one chart. The groups plotted become interested in you. | Second slide, related-work section |
| **Jenny Craig** | Before and after. The state of the art, then the state of the art with your work. | The one that carries the hire. Every statement and every talk |

The book's author writes proposals by building the Jenny Craig figure first, then
writing text to match it.

**Test.** Point at all three in the current materials. A candidate with no Jenny
Craig figure is asking the panel to do the comparison themselves.

**Trace.** Ch 9, the three kinds of figures.
**Effort** days. **Impact** high.

### Rule 14. Sell the margarita, not the blender

Reviewers and heads of school care about the future, not the provenance. You do
not sell a blender by describing its gears; you put a margarita on the box. Lead
with what the department gets. Put the method in the second half.

**Test.** Read the first sentence of every section. Count how many describe what
you did versus what the reader gets.

**Trace.** Ch 9, "To sell a blender, show a picture of a margarita".
**Effort** hours. **Impact** high.

### Rule 15. The four bad proposals are the four bad applications

| Archetype | The application version | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Social Security** | We published a lot, therefore hire us | Add the forward question and the tool that answers it |
| **Fishing Expedition** | An exciting question with no credible instrument | Name the instrument, or drop the question |
| **Big Toy Hammer** | A strong method looking for problems, all vaguely described | Commit to one problem the department cares about |
| **Vague** | Ambition without a checkable outcome | Add the Jenny Craig figure, which forces one |

The book's standard for a good proposal transfers directly: one well-posed
research question, matched to one research tool that will actually answer it,
supported by a qualified team and a before-and-after figure. Not address, not
make progress toward. Answer.

**Test.** Ask which of the four the draft is. Every weak draft is one of them.

**Trace.** Ch 9, four common kinds of bad proposals.
**Effort** an hour to diagnose. **Impact** high.

## D. Visibility and relationships, ranked by hiring impact

### Rule 16. Search results are the front door

Committees search candidates during the meeting. The book's author describes
sitting on a hiring committee where the group searched every candidate, and a
review panel that broke a deadlock using a proposer's website. The failure mode
is not absence, it is dilution: an old homepage, two stale profiles, a dormant
account, none of them current, none of them on message.

**Test.** Search the name in a clean browser session. Look at the first ten
results as a busy stranger would. Count how many are current and on message.

**Trace.** Ch 12, Googling yourself, and the research-website section.
**Effort** 2 hours to audit, a day to fix. **Impact** high, and cheap.

### Rule 17. The research site is checked after the shortlist meeting, not before

Treat it as the sign outside the shop. Fixed checklist, most of it from a science
journalist quoted in the book on what she needs within seconds:

- Name, current title, institution, contact, photograph (thumbnail and a
  high-resolution version)
- The one identity line, above the fold
- Three to five recent papers, linked
- Code, data, tools, the free samples from rule 12
- Teaching, if you are applying for teaching-weighted posts
- Plain layout. Dark text on light. Generous white space. Nothing that takes a
  second click to reach anything important

**Test.** Open it on a phone. Can a stranger state your identity line in ten
seconds?

**Trace.** Ch 12, your research website.
**Effort** a day. **Impact** medium to high.

### Rule 18. Move people down the funnel. Do not camp at its mouth

The funnel runs from people who have never heard of you, through those who have
and are unimpressed, to colleagues who think well of you, to the few who would
follow you anywhere. Broadcast reaches the mouth. Hiring happens at the narrow
end. The common mistake the book names is spending all the effort at the mouth
and never drawing anyone deeper.

Ratio discipline: promote other people's work roughly twelve times for each
self-promotion. Post things that are useful or entertaining to colleagues, not
status updates. One or two posts a day maximum, and fewer is fine.

**Test.** For each of the last ten public posts, which funnel stage did it serve?
A feed that is all mouth is a feed that is not working.

**Trace.** Ch 4, the marketing funnel. Ch 12, groups and pages, and the 12:1
ratio attributed to Chris Brogan.
**[ADAPTED]** Facebook groups and e-mail newsletters were the 2011 instruments.
Now: LinkedIn reaches academics in Europe, a maintained repository reaches the
computational subfield, and a seminar series or session you organise gives the
standing excuse to mail a few hundred relevant people something they are glad to
receive. The mechanism is unchanged.
**Effort** 1 to 2 hours a week. **Impact** medium. Real but slower than
positioning work, and it is where candidates over-invest.

### Rule 19. Conferences are won in the follow-up

"It's ALL about the follow-up. It's ONLY about the follow-up." Two touches:

1. Same day, a short note. Contact details, one line, "let us talk once we are
   both home". Its only job is to link your in-person and online selves.
2. One to two weeks later, when everyone is back at their desk, the substantive
   contact. This is the one candidates forget, because the conference is no
   longer there to remind them.

Everything else at a conference follows from being a good guest: introduce people
who look lost, ask the question that opens the room, take one good swing when
your topic comes up and then hand over the microphone.

**Test.** After the last conference, how many two-touch sequences were completed?
Usually zero, which is the finding.

**Trace.** Ch 10, the most important part of a meeting, citing Derek Sivers.
**Effort** 2 hours per conference. **Impact** high per hour spent.

### Rule 20. Cultivate a first and a second follower

Most people do not follow you, they follow your followers. Two people who will
publicly back you, cite you, invite you, show your slide, sit on a panel, write
the letter, are worth more than a hundred contacts. Treat them as equals, never
as underlings, or they do not stay.

**Test.** Name your first and second follower. If you cannot name them, that is
the most important gap in the campaign.

**Trace.** Ch 15, leadership, citing Derek Sivers's TED talk.
**Effort** months. **Impact** high. Start now because it cannot be rushed later.

### Rule 21. Ask a small favour before the big one

People who have done you a kindness like you more and are more willing to do
another. Asking for advice or an opinion gains twice: you learn something, and
you signal that you value their judgement. A small, genuine, easy-to-grant ask
should precede the large one by months.

The inverse matters too. If someone is being pushed toward harming you, stop the
process early, because harming you makes them like you less. Better to withdraw
than be rejected, better to keep a talk short than be asked to stop.

**Test.** For each intended referee, what small favour have you asked and had
granted in the last year?

**Trace.** Ch 4, the Benjamin Franklin effect, and schmoozing.
**Effort** minutes per ask. **Impact** high, compounding.

### Rule 22. The go-to test

Could a panellist who has never met you say "she is the one who does X"? Becoming
the go-to person in one small corner is, in the book's judgement, the usual route
to a junior faculty position, and it is what lets a referee reach for "best" and
"top two or three".

**Test.** Apply it literally. If the answer is no, positioning work outranks every
visibility action on the plan, because visibility without a position just spreads
a blur.

**Trace.** Ch 8, the go-to person, "this is the way I see most junior faculty get
hired".
**Effort** the diagnosis is free. **Impact** high, and it sequences the plan.

## E. Performance rules, applied in review mode

These are not standalone campaign actions. They govern the job talk, the
interview, and conference behaviour.

**The job talk arc** (Ch 11). Open by working against the preconception the room
has of you, whatever that is, and show a Beautiful Butterfly figure. Then a
Family Portrait for the experts. Then zoom out and tell how you got into the
field. Then the problem that broke the existing approach, with the before half of
the Jenny Craig figure. Then your SRI, named. Then the technical passage where
you go fast and address only the specialists. Then the after half of the figure.
Then the problems that remain.

**Do not be fully understood** (Ch 11). The book's author lost a faculty position
because the committee complained they understood the whole talk. First half
accessible, then step on the gas at the midpoint, then come back for the close.
Use depth spikes in the accessible half: one-line asides in subfield jargon that
tell the local expert you are one of them.

**Three and three** (Ch 11). Name the SRI at least three times, including once in
the question period. Show the key figure three times, and leave it on screen
during questions.

**Own the question period** (Ch 11). Prepare three or four memorable points about
the SRI. Answer amiable questions, then pivot: "what I want you to remember is".
A hostile or self-promoting question does not have to be answered, but it must be
responded to, and the pivot is the response.

**WIIFM at the poster and the coffee break** (Ch 10). The recorded failures are
all candidates answering with their own misfortunes: I printed it at the last
minute, it is not finished yet, my supervisor wants it by March. Humility aimed at
the wrong register reads as self-centred. Open with something the other person
already knows, name your SRI, and ask them a question.

**Trace.** Ch 10 and Ch 11 throughout.
**[ADAPTED]** The book's advice to prepare sound bites is kept, with its own
caveat: the author's prepared sound bites visibly annoyed an NPR reporter.
Prepared points work in a hostile technical room; openness works when the
stereotype to overcome is aloofness. Choose by which preconception you are facing.
