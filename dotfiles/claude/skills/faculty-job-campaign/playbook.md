# Playbook

The action catalogue for plan mode. Standing impact ranking, post archetypes with
worked examples, and the protocols that need a sequence rather than a rule.

Pull actions from here rather than inventing generic ones. Each entry carries its
effort and its standing hiring impact, which the plan can override for a
particular candidate if the reason is stated.

---

## Standing impact ranking

Highest first. When two actions compete for the same hours, the higher one wins
unless the diagnosis says otherwise.

| Tier | Actions |
|---|---|
| **1. Changes whether a shortlist happens** | Land the flagship paper the target market reads. Fix the positioning line. Secure the eligibility and the named first-PI scheme. Convert a saturated strand into the lead strand that matches the market. Close a hard eligibility blocker (professional registration, language, right to work). |
| **2. Changes how the file reads at the same evidence level** | The elimination audit and its fixes. The Jenny Craig figure. CV top-third rewrite. Research statement reframed as a white paper. Search-result audit. Referee cultivation. |
| **3. Compounds over one to two cycles** | First and second follower investment. Two-touch conference follow-up. The public artefact that is a real free sample. Seminar or session organising. Reviewing and panel service. |
| **4. Maintenance** | Website upkeep. Post cadence. Profile consistency. |
| **5. Skip during a campaign** | Personal logo. Video production. Press outreach. Public-engagement writing. Book projects. Any platform where the target panels are not present. |

A tier 4 action is worth doing only when tiers 1 to 3 have nothing outstanding
that fits the available hours.

## Post archetypes

For LinkedIn primarily, since that is where European academics and heads of
school are reachable. The same archetypes work on Bluesky and on a group mailing
list with the length adjusted.

Rules 18 and 21 govern the mix: roughly twelve posts serving other people for
each one serving you. That ratio is not modesty, it is what makes the twelfth
post land.

### Archetype 1: the useful artefact

You built something reusable and you are handing it over. This is rule 12 in post
form and it is the highest-value archetype, because it is the only one that gives
the reader something.

> Structure: what it does in one line, who it is for, the link, one figure or
> one code block, the limitation you already know about.

> **Example.** "We released the code for counting pedestrians, cyclists and
> e-scooters on a Raspberry Pi. Runs on-device, so no images leave the sensor.
> If you are doing active-travel counts and cannot get a camera permit, this is
> the route around it. [link] Caveat: the e-scooter class is still weak below
> 10 lux, which is the next thing we are fixing."

Length 80 to 150 words. One figure. Effort 30 minutes given the artefact exists.

### Archetype 2: the method note

A specific technical decision, why you made it, and what it cost. Not a summary
of your paper. The test is whether a reader in your subfield learns something
they could apply on Monday.

> **Example.** "Gradient boosting will happily predict that a road gets smoother
> without maintenance. We added monotonic constraints so the model cannot violate
> the physics, and lost about two points of test R-squared for it. Worth it: the
> unconstrained model was unusable for planning because engineers did not believe
> its outputs. Constraint set and results: [link]"

Length 100 to 200 words. Effort 45 minutes.

### Archetype 3: the field read

You read something and you are telling people what it means. This is the
promote-others archetype that earns the ratio, and the book's point about
becoming useful to reporters transfers exactly: promote other people's work
eleven times and the twelfth post about your own is welcome.

> **Example.** "Galaktionova and colleagues have a paper in Accident Analysis and
> Prevention on street vitality and traffic risk across Barcelona and Warsaw. The
> part worth your time is the multiscale treatment: the vitality-risk relationship
> reverses sign depending on the aggregation unit, which is a warning for anyone
> doing city-level safety regressions. [link]"

Length 60 to 120 words. Effort 20 minutes. Do this most often.

### Archetype 4: the result with a before and after

Your Jenny Craig figure, in public. Previous state of the art, then yours. One
figure, two panels or two lines.

> **Example.** "Current practice estimates cyclist exposure from annual manual
> counts at a handful of junctions. Left panel: what a planner sees today. Right
> panel: the same corridor with continuous multimodal counts over eight weeks.
> The peak they were designing for is on a different day of the week. Paper: [link]"

Length 80 to 150 words. Effort 1 hour including the figure. Use sparingly, this
one is self-promotion and it spends ratio.

### Archetype 5: the invitation

You are organising something and you want people there. A session, a seminar, a
workshop, a call for a dataset. The book's point about community service that
gives you a standing reason to write to several hundred relevant people.

> **Example.** "We are running a session on edge AI for active travel monitoring
> at [conference]. Looking for contributions on low-cost sensing, privacy-preserving
> counting, and validation against manual counts. Abstracts by [date]: [link].
> Happy to talk through a fit if you are unsure."

Length 60 to 100 words. Effort 20 minutes for the post, days for the thing.
Impact medium to high, because organising is esteem evidence a panel can see.

### What not to post

| Pattern | Why |
|---|---|
| "Delighted to share that our paper is out" with no content | Serves you, teaches nothing, spends ratio for no return |
| Conference selfies with no substance | Funnel mouth only |
| Reposts with no added commentary | Adds nothing the original did not have |
| Anything about the job search itself | Reads as the orphan archetype, rule 10 |
| Grievance posts about review or funding | Same, and panels remember them |
| More than two posts a day | Reads as someone not doing research |

## Profile and photograph standards

Rule 16 and 17 in checklist form. All of this is cheap and most candidates lose
points here for free.

**Photograph.** One image used everywhere: website, LinkedIn, ORCID, institutional
page, conference profiles. Recognition depends on it being the same one. Plain
background, upper body, eye contact with the lens, natural light, no conference
lanyard, no cropped group photo. Dress one notch above what the target department
wears. Two versions: a thumbnail and a high-resolution file a press office or
conference organiser can take without asking.

**The bio ladder.** Write three lengths once and reuse them. All three open with
the identity line from rule 4.

- 25 words for a conference programme or a session chair
- 75 words for a seminar invitation or a panel
- 150 words for a website or a funding application

**Profile consistency.** Same job title, same institution, same identity line
across every property. A panel that finds three different job titles reads it as
carelessness. Audit list: personal site, institutional page, Google Scholar,
ORCID, LinkedIn, GitHub, ResearchGate if it exists, old institutional pages that
are still live.

**Prune.** Dormant properties that rank above live ones are the real cost. Either
update, redirect, or take them down.

## Per-paper promotion protocol

Runs once per paper, on acceptance. Total effort about 3 hours. Do it in this
order.

1. **Before submission**: check the paper names your SRI, in the same words you
   use everywhere else (rule 8).
2. **On acceptance, day 0**: update the CV, the website publication list, Google
   Scholar, ORCID. Fifteen minutes, and it is the thing that keeps the search
   results current.
3. **Day 0**: submit the Beautiful Butterfly figure as cover art if the journal
   takes one. The book's point is that journals want them and do not pay for
   them, so acceptance rates are better than people assume, and a cover is a CV
   line either way.
4. **Day 1**: direct mail, not broadcast. Write individually to the people who
   will actually care. The safe default list is the authors you cited: they will
   at minimum be pleased to see their names in your reference list. Three
   sentences each, no attachment, a link.
5. **Day 2**: the archetype 4 post, with the before-and-after figure.
6. **Week 1**: put the code, data or tool up if it is not already up, with the
   paper linked from the README and the README linked from the paper.
7. **Week 2**: add the finding to your standing talk. A paper that never enters
   the talk was written for the CV only.

Skip the press office unless the institution asks. Rule set exclusion: press
coverage does not move panels.

## Conference protocol

Effort about 4 hours beyond attending. Impact high per hour.

**Before.** Pick three people you want to know, from the programme, not from
fame. Look up what they are presenting. Have one real question ready for each.
Know your own identity line cold, because you will say it forty times.

**During.**
- Sit near the front when your topic comes up. Ask one good question, then let
  others have the floor (rule set E).
- At coffee breaks and poster sessions, find the person standing alone. This is
  both kind and effective, and the book is specific that a room full of
  uncomfortable scientists is an opportunity.
- When asked about your work, open with something they already know, name the
  SRI, then ask them something (rules 5 and 22, and the WIIFM failure in E).
- Collect contact details and write them down the same hour.

**After, and this is the part that matters.**
- Same day: the short note. Contact details, one line, "let us talk properly once
  we are both home". Its whole job is linking your in-person and online selves.
- Day 10 to 14: the substantive follow-up. A specific thing: a paper of theirs
  you read, a dataset you could share, a question their talk raised, a session
  you are organising.

Track it. A spreadsheet with name, met where, touch 1 date, touch 2 date. Without
a list, touch 2 does not happen, and touch 2 is the entire value of the trip.

## Referee and follower cultivation

Rules 20 and 21. This is the slowest action in the catalogue and the one that
cannot be compressed later, so it starts in week 1 regardless of what else is on
the plan.

**Map first.** Three groups, named individuals in each:

| Group | What you need from them | How many |
|---|---|---|
| Referees | Letters that use superlatives | 3, at least one outside your own institutions |
| First and second followers | Public backing: citing, inviting, showing your slide, naming you in a room | 2 |
| Panel-adjacent | People who sit on panels in your target departments and who would recognise your name | 4 to 6 |

The three groups overlap. A referee who never mentions you in a room is a weaker
asset than a follower who is not writing a letter.

**The sequence, per person, over 6 to 12 months.**

1. Make yourself known for something specific. They should be able to complete
   "the one who does X" before any ask.
2. Do something unasked that helps them: review a paper promptly, send a student
   their way, flag their work in an archetype 3 post, share a dataset.
3. Ask a small favour: read two pages, an opinion on a plan, a question about
   their method. Small enough to grant in twenty minutes. This is the Benjamin
   Franklin move, and the point is that granting it makes them more disposed
   toward you, not less (rule 21).
4. Give them something to back publicly: a preprint worth citing, a tool worth
   using, a session invitation.
5. Only then, the large ask: the letter, the collaboration, the nomination.

**Referee hygiene.** Ask at least six weeks before a deadline. Send a one-page
brief: the post, the criteria in the advertisement, the two or three things you
would like emphasised, your current CV. A referee who has to invent the emphasis
writes a generic letter. Rule 10 applies: give them material they can turn into
"best" or "top two or three".

**Warning sign.** If you cannot name your first and second follower, no amount of
posting fixes it. That is a rule 20 gap and it goes at the top of the plan.

## The teaching and service side

Underweighted by the book, which assumes a US research-university career.
Included because it is scored explicitly in UK, Irish, Dutch and Portuguese
advertisements.

| Action | Effort | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Two-page teaching philosophy, with evidence from courses actually taught | 1 day | High for teaching-weighted posts, medium elsewhere. Often requested and often missing |
| Course evaluations collected, translated if needed, summarised into one table | Hours | Medium. Turns a claim into evidence |
| A named course you could deliver from day one, matched to the department's gap | Hours per application | High. This is the head of school's concern from rule 2 |
| Supervision record, stated with numbers and outcomes | Hours | Medium |
| National teaching accreditation where the market expects it (UK and Irish schemes) | Weeks | Medium. Check whether the advertisement names it before spending the time |
| Journal reviewing and proposal evaluation, stated with counts | Already done, state it | Medium. Esteem evidence a panel can verify |
| Organising a session or seminar series | Days | Medium to high. Esteem plus the standing mailing-list excuse from rule 18 |
